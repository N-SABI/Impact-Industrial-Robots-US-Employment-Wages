# Impact-Industrial-Robots-US-Employment-Wages

This project replicates the seminal study by Acemoglu and Restrepo (2017), "Robots and Jobs: Evidence from US Labor Markets," which examines the impact of industrial robot adoption on employment and wages across U.S. commuting zones, and extends the analysis with additional econometric specifications.

The replication reconstructs the exposure-to-robots measure (Bartik-style approach combining industry-level employment shares with the adjusted penetration of robots computed from technologically advanced European countries, i.e. EURO5), and estimates long-differences and stacked-differences regressions as well as instrumental variable (2SLS) models. The replicated results confirm the original findings: one additional robot per thousand workers reduces the local employment-to-population ratio by 0.2 percentage points and wages by 0.42%, with stronger effects for men and concentration in manufacturing and routine manual occupations. Pretrends analysis and robustness checks (controlling for imports, offshoring, and regional fixed effects) confirm the causal validity of these results.

The project's own contribution extends this framework through three additional econometric specifications: a degree-2 polynomial regression on robot exposure to capture potential non-linear effects; a model interacting exposure with an age indicator (above/below 65) to test whether older workers are differentially affected; and a model interacting exposure with education levels (high school, college, master's), which reveals that automation mainly penalizes middle-skilled workers while unexpectedly benefiting the most highly educated workers (master's and doctoral degree holders), particularly men.

Together, these analyses—disaggregated by gender, education level, and industry—highlight a polarization of the U.S. labor market driven by robotization, and underscore the need for targeted public policy (training, reskilling) for the most exposed workers.
